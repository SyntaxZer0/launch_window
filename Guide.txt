===============================================================================
                    L A U N C H   W I N D O W
        a field manual for the scientist who wants to leave home
===============================================================================

You are not the pilot. There is no throttle, no autopilot, no glowing
"prograde" marker begging you to burn. There are worlds out there — one of
them your target — a blank notebook, and the laws of physics, which do not
negotiate and do not round in your favour.

Your job is to MEASURE what you are not told, DERIVE what you cannot see,
and COMMIT a launch. A real physics engine then flies your rocket under
your star's gravity. If your arithmetic was right, you arrive. If it
wasn't, the void will tell you exactly how you were wrong, and invite you
to try again.

This manual teaches the method, not the answers. Every game (every "seed")
hides different numbers, so there is nothing to memorise — only a chain of
reasoning to walk. Grab paper. Old-school astronomers drew their solar
system first; you should too.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  RUNNING IT
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    ./run.sh                one window, a fresh random universe
    ./run.sh 42             seed 42 — hand a friend the same number and you
                            both get the same solar system to race
    python3 tui.py 42       the same, without the launcher script

Everything lives in ONE window. On the left: the SYSTEM MAP (the star, your
home world, and every other world, all orbiting live) above the MISSION
CONSOLE, where you type commands. Down the right: the GUIDE (this text), your
NOTES, and a CALCULATOR — all open at once, so you can read, jot and compute
side by side. Panels toggle with ^o/^g/^n/^b and zoom to full-screen with ^z;
^s saves everything.

You drive the mission by typing in the console:
    measure                 print every raw instrument reading
    target [n]              list the worlds / pick which one to reach
    rocket <v_e> <mr>       build your rocket
    add / del / clear / plan   edit your flight plan (see below)
    play / pause / speed / warp   run the mission clock (see below)
    fly                     launch, at the current day
    key                     the answer key (spoilers, for checking afterwards)
    help                    the full command list

You get unlimited attempts; each 'fly' draws your trajectory and diagnoses any
error. Nothing is graded until you commit a burn.

A note on units: work in METRES and SECONDS the whole way through and the
formulas below just work. The instruments report periods in DAYS to be
readable — multiply by 86400 to get seconds before you compute anything.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  CHOOSING A TARGET
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Your star has several worlds, all orbiting outward of home. Type 'target' to
list them with their distances and years; 'target <n>' (or a name) aims your
mission at one. Your chosen world glows red on the map (the others sit muted),
and its name shows in the status bar.

Everything below — the window, the injection, the capture, the fuel — is
computed for the world you are aiming at. Farther worlds need a bigger, slower
transfer and far more dv, so the real question becomes: which of these can I
actually reach with the rocket I can build? Switch targets and you must start
the sums again — it is a different journey each time.

The worlds vary widely in their own right, too: a small dense rock with gentle
gravity is cheap to leave; a big heavy one demands a much larger rocket.
Measure the one you mean to visit.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  THE CHAIN OF REASONING
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Everything unlocks in order. Each measurement feeds the next calculation.
Get one wrong early and the error rides silently all the way to a missed
rendezvous — which is precisely the point.

. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
  STEP 1 — HOW BIG IS YOUR WORLD?     (measure)
. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
Two ground stations a known distance d apart, north-south, read the sun's
angle at the same moment. The angles differ by dtheta. That angular gap is
the same fraction of a full circle as d is of the whole circumference. This
is Eratosthenes, ~240 BC, and it still works:

        circumference  C = d * (360 / dtheta)          [dtheta in degrees]
        radius         R = C / (2*pi)

. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
  STEP 2 — HOW HEAVY IS YOUR WORLD?   (measure, then Step 1)
. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
Drop a weight from height h; it takes time t to fall. From rest under
constant acceleration, h = (1/2) g t^2, so:

        surface gravity  g = 2h / t^2

Surface gravity is just the pull of the planet's mass at its surface,
g = G*M / R^2. You don't need M and G separately — the useful bundle is
the "gravitational parameter" mu = G*M:

        mu_home = g * R^2

(That bundle is all orbital mechanics ever needs. You will compute a mu for
your planet, your star, and the target.)

If you ever want a raw escape speed from the surface: v = sqrt(2*mu/R).

. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
  STEP 3 — HOW BIG IS THE SOLAR SYSTEM?   (measure)
. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
This is the beautiful part, and it's how it really happened historically:
you can know the SHAPE of the whole system long before its SIZE.

(a) The sidereal clock gives both worlds' orbital periods, T1 (yours) and
    T2 (the target's). Kepler's third law says period^2 is proportional to
    orbit-radius^3 around the same star. So the RATIO of the orbits falls
    straight out, with no distances at all:

        r2 / r1 = (T2 / T1)^(2/3)

    Now you know the target's orbit is, say, 1.6x yours — but 1.6x of WHAT?
    You still have no absolute distance. The map has no scale bar.

(b) Radar fixes the scale bar. Fired at "opposition" (the moment the target
    is closest, straight out from the star past you), the echo's round-trip
    time t_rt gives one real distance in metres — the gap between the two
    orbits:

        r2 - r1 = c * t_rt / 2

(c) Two facts, two unknowns. Combine them:

        r1 = (r2 - r1) / ( (r2/r1) - 1 )
        r2 = (r2/r1) * r1

    The instant you have one absolute distance, the entire system snaps to
    scale. This is a genuinely great moment; savour it.

(d) With a real radius and period for your own orbit, Kepler gives the
    STAR's mass parameter:

        mu_sun = 4 * pi^2 * r1^3 / T1^2

    (Sanity check: plug in r2 and T2; you must get the same mu_sun. If you
    don't, a number upstream is wrong.)

. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
  STEP 4 — HOW HEAVY IS THE TARGET?   (measure)
. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
You'll need the target's mass to STOP there (a capture burn). A moon is a
free scale for whatever it orbits — Kepler a third time:

        mu_target = 4 * pi^2 * a_moon^3 / T_moon^2

where a_moon is the moon's orbit radius and T_moon its period (in seconds!).

. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
  STEP 5 — THE TRANSFER ELLIPSE       (pure maths now — no instruments)
. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
The cheapest path between two circular orbits is a Hohmann transfer: an
ellipse that just kisses your orbit at its near end and the target's orbit
at its far end. Its semi-major axis is the average of the two radii:

        a_t = (r1 + r2) / 2

Speeds come from the "vis-viva" equation, v = sqrt( mu * (2/r - 1/a) ):

        v1     = sqrt(mu_sun / r1)                  your circular speed
        v2     = sqrt(mu_sun / r2)                  target's circular speed
        v_peri = sqrt(mu_sun * (2/r1 - 1/a_t))      transfer speed at r1
        v_apo  = sqrt(mu_sun * (2/r2 - 1/a_t))      transfer speed at r2

Your prograde INJECTION burn (the first maneuver in your flight plan --
"day 0 prograde ...") is just how much faster than your own orbit you must
go at departure:

        injection dv = v_peri - v1

And the coast takes exactly half the ellipse's period:

        transfer time  tau = pi * sqrt( a_t^3 / mu_sun )

. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
  STEP 6 — WHEN TO LAUNCH (the "window")   (watch the live phase readout)
. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
You do not aim at where the target IS. You aim at where it WILL BE after
your transfer time tau. During tau the target sweeps forward by an angle
2*pi*tau/T2. For it to arrive at your ellipse's far point exactly when you
do, it must LEAD you at launch by:

        phi_required = pi - 2*pi*tau/T2       (radians; = 180deg - ...)

The ephemeris tells you tonight's lead, phi0. Both worlds run counter-
clockwise; your inner orbit is faster, so you catch up on the target and
the lead shrinks at the relative rate (w1 - w2), where w = 2*pi/T:

        w1 = 2*pi/T1,   w2 = 2*pi/T2       (w1 > w2)

Wait until the shrinking lead equals phi_required:

        t_launch = ( (phi0 - phi_required) mod 2*pi ) / (w1 - w2)

That's your launch time in seconds from "tonight" (divide by 86400 for the
days the console wants). Miss it and the next identical alignment is one
SYNODIC PERIOD later:

        S = 1 / | 1/T1 - 1/T2 |

Blow a window and you can wait a long time for the next. Measure twice.

The status bar shows phi0 LIVE — the target's current lead — ticking as the
mission clock rolls. You can literally watch the lead shrink toward
phi_required and launch as it arrives. See THE MISSION CLOCK below for how to
speed time up, slow it down, or jump straight to your launch day.

. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
  STEP 7 — CAN YOUR ROCKET AFFORD IT?     (rocket command)
. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
Two burns cost fuel: leaving home and stopping at the target. You do each
from a low "parking" orbit 300 km up. The parking speed there is:

        v_park = sqrt( mu_body / (R_body + 300000) )        per body

The clever bit (the Oberth effect): burning deep in a gravity well is
cheaper than the raw heliocentric dv suggests. The real burn to reach a
hyperbolic "excess" speed v_inf from a parking orbit is:

        burn = sqrt( v_park^2 + v_inf^2 ) - v_park

        departure burn:  v_inf = injection dv   (uses mu_home, R_home)
        capture burn:    v_inf = (v2 - v_apo)    (uses mu_target, R_target)

        total dv needed = departure burn + capture burn

Finally, Tsiolkovsky's rocket equation turns dv into fuel. With exhaust
velocity v_e and mass ratio m0/mf (fuelled over dry):

        dv_capacity = v_e * ln( m0 / mf )

So the mass ratio you must build to afford your mission is:

        m0/mf = exp( total_dv_needed / v_e )

Build with a little margin. Under-measure your planet's mass back in Step 2
and you'll under-build here, and find out at the worst possible moment.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  COMMITTING THE LAUNCH — THE FLIGHT PLAN
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
You don't fire a single burn any more. You write a FLIGHT PLAN: an ordered
list of maneuvers, each one a TRIGGER (when it fires) and a BURN (how hard,
which way). The plan runs top to bottom — it waits for maneuver 1's trigger,
burns, then waits for maneuver 2's, and so on. This gives you the freedom to
reach the target however you like, and makes the mission far harder to stumble
into by trial and error (there are now many continuous knobs, not two).

THE MISSION CLOCK. Time is always moving. The status bar shows the current day
and the live phase. You worked out in Step 6 the lead you need and the day it
happens; your job is to be READY — rocket built, plan written — and launch when
the sky lines up. Control time however you like:

    play / pause     start or stop the clock  (or spacebar, with map focused)
    speed <x>        set the time rate  (or +/- ; from x0.25 up to x64)
    warp <day>       jump the clock forward to a day  ('launch <day>' is a
                     synonym)

Time only moves FORWARD. Pausing to compute is fine and encouraged, but if you
blow past your window you must wait (or warp) to the next one. 'fly' launches
at the current day — so warp to your launch day (or let it roll there) and fly.

TRIGGERS (when a burn fires):
    day N        at mission day N   (day 0 = the instant you release)
    radius R     when your distance from the STAR reaches R metres
                 (this is your "height"; accepts AU, e.g.  radius 1.5au)
    apo          at the high point of your current arc (apoapsis)
    peri         at the low point (periapsis)
    near D       when you come within D metres of the target

DIRECTIONS (which way to burn):
    prograde     along your motion   (speeds up, raises the far side)
    retrograde   against your motion (slows down — braking and capture)
    out / in     away from / toward the star (radial)
    heading A    an absolute angle, A degrees (0 = the +x reference line)

Console commands:  add <trigger> <dir> <dv>   del <n>   clear   plan
                   warp <day>   play / pause / speed <x>   target <n>
                   fly   save   key   help

The simplest valid plan IS the Hohmann you computed above — one line:

        warp 295                       (roll the clock to your Step-6 day)
        add day 0 prograde 3234        (your Step-5 injection dv)
        fly

But now you can be cleverer — split the injection, trim at apoapsis, or bend
in with a braking burn as you arrive, e.g.

        add day 0 prograde 3000        get most of the way onto the transfer
        add apo prograde 250           raise the far side at apoapsis
        add near 3e9 retrograde 400    brake as the target looms

FUEL (it rewards good flying):
    * Your rocket's total dv (Step 7) is the budget; each burn spends its dv.
    * The FIRST burn (still in the home well) and any burn INSIDE the target's
      sphere get the Oberth discount — cheaper than their face dv. Deep-space
      burns cost full price. The flight log tags which is which.
    * CAPTURE happens when you enter the sphere of influence slowly enough that
      your leftover dv can hold you. So a well-placed 'near ... retrograde'
      arrival burn genuinely helps — arrive gently and you need less fuel.

It integrates your path for real, animates the ship along it, and reports the
flight log, closest approach vs the sphere of influence, and — if you missed —
a diagnosis:
    * "arc fell short / overshot"     -> your prograde dv is too low / high
    * "grazed the SOI but too fast"   -> arrive slower, or carry more dv
    * "reaches the orbit, wrong time" -> fix the LAUNCH day, or add a
                                         'near <D> retrograde' arrival burn

Plans are saved per world-system: type 'save' (or ^s) and your plan, notes,
calculator variables, chosen target and progress are written to saves/. They
load again next time you open that seed.

A QUICK TEST (seed 42): run  ./run.sh 42  (or  python3 tui.py 42 ). Build a
rocket with exhaust velocity v_e = 4000 and mass ratio = 1.6 (about 1880 m/s
of dv), then:  warp 294.99 ;  add day 0 prograde 3234.3 ;  fly.  It tolerates
about +/-2.5 days and +/-60 m/s, and you make orbit around day 188. (The exact
launch day and injection for ANY seed, and any target, are in the answer key —
type 'key'.)

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  A WORKED SKELETON (symbols, so it spoils nothing)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    R      = d * 360 / dtheta / (2*pi)
    g      = 2*h / t^2
    mu_h   = g * R^2
    ratio  = (T2/T1)^(2/3)                         [T's in seconds]
    r1     = (c*t_rt/2) / (ratio - 1)
    r2     = ratio * r1
    mu_sun = 4*pi^2 * r1^3 / T1^2
    mu_t   = 4*pi^2 * a_moon^3 / T_moon^2
    a_t    = (r1 + r2)/2
    dv_inj = sqrt(mu_sun*(2/r1 - 1/a_t)) - sqrt(mu_sun/r1)
    tau    = pi * sqrt(a_t^3 / mu_sun)
    phi_req= pi - 2*pi*tau/T2
    t_lnch = ((phi0 - phi_req) mod 2pi) / (2*pi/T1 - 2*pi/T2)
    ... then the two Oberth burns, sum, and Tsiolkovsky for the mass ratio.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  WHAT THE SIMULATOR ASSUMES (so you know it isn't cheating)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  * Orbits are circular and in one plane. (Real orbits are elliptical and
    tilted; those add a plane-change burn and a harder window — a natural
    "hard mode" to build later.)
  * There are several target worlds, all on circular orbits OUTWARD of home;
    you pick one with 'target'. (Inner worlds, which need an inward transfer,
    and gravity assists BETWEEN worlds are natural things to add later.)
  * The rocket's coasting path feels only the STAR's gravity ("patched
    conics"). Every world's mass still fully sets your fuel bill via the two
    burns above — the worlds just don't tug the coast.
  * Burns are instantaneous.
These are the standard first approximations real mission designers start
from. They are honest: every formula above is exactly what the integrator
obeys, so correct maths always arrives. The answer key ('key') exists so you
can check each number afterwards and find where a wrong turn crept in.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  IF YOU'RE SHOWING SOMEONE
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Start them on a shared seed. Have them draw the two orbits and the star on
paper first, mark tonight's lead angle, then work the chain top to bottom.
The magic beat is Step 3(c): the moment the radar ping turns a dimensionless
sketch into a real map with kilometres on it. Everything after that is
"just" careful sums — and then a rocket, flown honestly, proves you right.
